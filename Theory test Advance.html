Part 1

Multiple-choice questions

How it works

  • You have 57 minutes to answer 50 questions. A question and several possible answers appear on the screen, and you select the right answer.
  • 3 questions are about one short video (up to 30 seconds) showing normal driving, such as a town centre or a country road. The video is silent and you can watch it as many times as you like.
  • You can flag questions to come back to, and go back to change any answer at any point.
  • You can finish once you have answered every question. You do not have to use the full 57 minutes.
  • You need 43 out of 50 (86%) to pass this part.

Where the questions come from

The questions are based on three official books. Most of what you need is in the free online Highway Code and Know Your Traffic Signs.

  • The Highway Code: rules, signals, crossings, motorways, first aid.
  • Know Your Traffic Signs: signs, road markings and signals.
  • Driving – the essential skills: technique, hazards, eco-driving, maintenance.

New for 2026: the car test now includes CPR questions and, for the first time, questions on using a defibrillator (AED). DVSA says the change adds no extra time, cost or difficulty.

Part 2

Hazard perception

How it works

  • First you watch a short video about how this part works.
  • Then you watch 14 clips of everyday road scenes. Each has at least one developing hazard, and one clip has two, so there are 15 hazards.
  • Click as soon as you see a hazard starting to develop. You can score up to 5 points for each one. The earlier you click, the more you score.
  • You do not lose points for clicking when nothing develops. But if you click continuously or in a pattern, you score zero for that clip.
  • You get one attempt at each clip and cannot review or change your responses.
  • You need 44 out of 75 to pass this part.

What counts as a developing hazard?

Something that would make you take action, such as changing speed or direction.

DVSA's own example: a car parked at the side of the road is not a developing hazard. When you get closer, its right indicator starts to flash and it starts to move away. You would need to slow down, so it is now a developing hazard.

Each hazard has a scoring window that opens when it starts to develop. Clicking early in the window scores 5, and later clicks score 4, 3, 2 or 1. A click before the window opens scores nothing but costs nothing, so click again as the hazard develops.

Test day

What to take and what happens at the centre

Take with you

  • Your UK photocard driving licence.
  • Northern Ireland licence: the photocard and the paper counterpart.
  • Paper licence: bring a valid passport as well.
  • Changed your name since booking: bring proof such as a marriage certificate or deed poll.

If you do not bring the right things, your test is cancelled and you do not get your money back.

Arriving

  • Arrive 15 minutes before your test. If you are late, the test is cancelled and you lose your fee.
  • There is no waiting area for anyone who comes with you.
  • Turn off phones, smartwatches and fitness trackers before you go in.
  • Personal items go in a locker: bags, coats, hats, earphones, phones, watches, wallets, pens and anything in your pockets.

Checks

  • Staff check your face against your photo ID.
  • Security check: show your pockets are empty, roll up your sleeves, pat yourself down, show the top of your socks and your ears, and stand still for a metal detector.
  • Cheating is illegal: you can be sent to prison and banned from driving.
After the test

Results, pass certificate and resits

If you pass

You get a letter with a pass certificate number. You need it to book your driving test. It lasts 2 years; pass the practical in that time or you must retake the theory test.

If you fail

The letter shows which part you did not score enough on. You must book and take both parts again, even if you passed one, and wait at least 3 working days.

Booking

The test costs £23 and you need a provisional licence to book. The test is only available in English, Welsh or British Sign Language.

Support

If you have a reading difficulty, disability or health condition

Ask for support when you book. You can hear the test through headphones in English or Welsh. With proof of a reading difficulty you can get extra time, someone to read the screen and record your answers, or someone to reword questions. Deaf candidates can take the test in BSL (a BSL video appears on screen) or bring a BSL interpreter or lip speaker at no extra cost, and hearing loops are available. For anything else, contact DVSA before booking: 0300 200 1122 or theorycustomerservices@dvsa.gov.uk.

Technique

Exam technique that saves marks

  • Budget about a minute per question. 57 minutes for 50 questions leaves time for a review at the end.
  • Read every word, including all four answers, before choosing. Distractors are often nearly right.
  • Flag and move on if you're stuck, then use the review screen to finish the flagged ones.
  • For the video questions, watch the whole clip once before reading the questions, then replay to check details such as signs, road type, weather and gaps.
  • Hazard perception: scan near and far, left, right and ahead. Click when something first shows it may develop, then click again as it develops.
  • Keep clicks natural: a click or two per hazard, spaced out. Rapid or rhythmic clicking scores zero for the clip.
  • Use the break to reset if part 1 felt hard. Part 2 is scored separately.
  • Use the numbers: stopping distances, speed limits and distances come up often. See Key numbers.

Lights, horn and arm signals

  • Headlights: must be used when visibility is seriously reduced, generally less than 100 metres. Use dipped headlights at night in built-up areas and in dull daytime weather.
  • Fog lights: only when visibility is seriously reduced. Switch them off when it improves.
  • Hazard warning lights: when stationary, to warn you are temporarily obstructing traffic, or briefly on motorways and unrestricted dual carriageways to warn of a hazard or queue ahead.
  • Flashing headlights: only to let others know you are there.
  • Horn: only while moving, to warn others you are there. Never when stationary, or in a built-up area between 11.30 pm and 7 am, unless another road user poses a danger.
  • Right arm straight out: I intend to move out to the right or turn right.
  • Right arm rotating anticlockwise: I intend to move in to the left or turn left.
  • Right arm moving up and down: I intend to slow down or stop.
  • Police stopping you: they may flash blue lights or headlights, sound a siren or horn and point or indicate left. Pull over and stop when it is safe.
  • Beacons: flashing blue, red or green lights mean emergency vehicles (green is used by doctors). Amber beacons mean slow-moving or stationary vehicles, or traffic officers.
